The EU AI Act does not impose one generic transparency notice on every artificial-intelligence system. It assigns different controls to different actors and use cases. A provider may need to design a system that informs people they are interacting with AI or marks synthetic output in a machine-readable format. A deployer may need to tell exposed people that emotion recognition is operating or label a deepfake when it is published.

Confusing those duties creates a practical gap. A visible label does not replace provider-side marking, and an embedded technical signal does not necessarily inform the person seeing the content.

So the operational rule runs in that order: classify the organisation's role and the Article 50 trigger, then choose the transparency control.

## Read the Legal Status First

[Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02024R1689-20260727), the EU AI Act, is binding law. Article 50 has applied since **2 August 2026**. The European Commission's [July 2026 guidelines](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/guidelines-transparency-obligations-providers-and-deployers-ai-systems) explain how the Commission interprets the provision, but the guidelines are non-binding and courts retain authority over interpretation.

The Act's transparency layers also have different timelines:

<div className="overflow-x-auto">

| Rule | Status on 23 August 2026 | Practical meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Article 50 transparency duties | Binding and applicable | Most provider and deployer duties have applied since 2 August 2026. |
| Article 50(2) for generative systems already on the market | Binding transition | Providers of systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 must meet the machine-readable marking duty by 2 December 2026. |
| GPAI-provider duties | Binding and applicable, subject to transition | Chapter V has applied since 2 August 2025; models placed on the market before that date must comply by 2 August 2027. |
| Annex III high-risk duties | Binding, later application | The amended timeline applies the main high-risk requirements from 2 December 2027. |
| Annex I product-system high-risk duties | Binding, later application | The amended timeline applies the main high-risk requirements from 2 August 2028. |

</div>

This is an EU-level view. It does not resolve national enforcement arrangements, sector-specific rules, or parallel obligations under data-protection, consumer, employment, media, accessibility, or copyright law.

## Classify the Role Before the System

The Act defines a **provider** as the person or organisation that develops an AI system or general-purpose AI model, or has one developed, and places it on the market or puts it into service under its own name or trademark. A **deployer** uses an AI system under its authority in a professional activity.

Those roles are not permanent labels for a company. The same organisation can be a deployer of one system and a provider of another. A distributor, importer, deployer, or other third party can also become the provider of a high-risk system by rebranding it, substantially modifying it, or changing its intended purpose so that it becomes high-risk.

Territorial scope is broader than establishment alone. The Act covers providers placing systems or GPAI models on the Union market, EU-based deployers, and third-country providers or deployers where system output is used in the Union. Purely personal, non-professional use is excluded from deployer obligations.

## The Four Article 50 Triggers

Article 50 addresses four practical situations. The responsible actor, delivery mechanism, and exceptions differ in each one.

<div className="overflow-x-auto">

| Trigger | Actor | Required action | Timing | Principal qualification or exception |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AI intended to interact directly with a natural person | Provider | Design and develop the system so the person is informed that they are interacting with AI. | No later than the first interaction | No notice where the AI nature is obvious to a reasonably well-informed, observant, and circumspect person in context; limited law-enforcement exception. |
| AI, including a GPAI system, generating synthetic audio, image, video, or text | Provider | Mark output in a machine-readable format and make artificial generation or manipulation detectable using effective, interoperable, robust, and reliable technical solutions as far as technically feasible. | In the generated output | Excludes standard-editing assistance or changes that do not substantially alter the deployer's input or its semantics; limited law-enforcement exception. |
| Emotion recognition or biometric categorisation | Deployer | Inform exposed natural persons that the system is operating and process personal data under applicable EU data-protection law. | No later than first exposure | Limited exception for legally permitted criminal-investigation uses with safeguards; prohibited practices remain prohibited. |
| Deepfake image, audio, or video | Deployer | Disclose that the content was artificially generated or manipulated. | No later than first exposure | Artistic, creative, satirical, fictional, or analogous works may use an appropriate disclosure that does not hamper display or enjoyment; limited law-enforcement exception. |
| AI-generated or manipulated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest | Deployer | Disclose that the text was artificially generated or manipulated. | No later than first exposure | No Article 50(4) label where the content underwent human review or editorial control and a natural or legal person holds editorial responsibility; limited law-enforcement exception. |

</div>

All human-facing information under Article 50 must be clear, distinguishable, and accessible. Timing matters: a disclosure hidden only in a general policy page may be too remote or too late to inform someone at the first interaction or exposure.

### 1. Direct Interaction: Test the Context, Not the Interface Label

The provider duty covers systems intended to interact directly with natural persons. A chatbot, voice assistant, avatar, or conversational interface may therefore need an AI notice built into its first interaction.

The exception for an "obvious" AI interaction is contextual. The legal test is not whether the development team knows the interface is automated. It is whether a reasonably well-informed, observant, and circumspect person would find the AI nature obvious in the circumstances and context of use. Audience, channel, branding, language, and the realism of the interaction can all affect that assessment.

A cautious implementation records why the interaction is considered obvious or shows the notice before relying on the exception.

### 2. Synthetic Content: Marking Is a Provider Control

Providers of systems that generate synthetic audio, images, video, or text must make the output detectable as artificially generated or manipulated and mark it in a machine-readable format. The solution must be effective, interoperable, robust, and reliable as far as technically feasible, taking account of the content type, implementation cost, and generally acknowledged state of the art.

This is a technical value-chain control. Requirements should address how a mark is created, attached, preserved through normal processing, and exposed to detection tools. Acceptance tests should cover the supported modalities and export paths rather than only the system's primary user interface.

The standard-editing exception is narrow. It applies where the AI performs an assistive function for standard editing or does not substantially alter the deployer's input or its semantics. Organisations relying on it need a documented boundary between assistance and substantive generation or manipulation.

### 3. Emotion and Biometric Analysis: Disclosure Does Not Cure a Prohibition

Deployers of emotion-recognition or biometric-categorisation systems must inform people exposed to the operation of the system. They must also process personal data in accordance with applicable EU data-protection law.

That notice is not an authorization. Article 5 prohibits some uses, including certain emotion inference in workplaces and education and biometric categorisation based on sensitive or protected characteristics. A prohibited use cannot be made lawful by adding a disclosure.

The deployment review should therefore test legality first and transparency second.

### 4. Published Content: Labels Depend on the Use

Deployers must disclose deepfake images, audio, or video. The Act defines a deepfake as AI-generated or manipulated content that resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities, or events and would falsely appear authentic or truthful.

For evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional, or analogous works, disclosure is still required but may be made in an appropriate manner that does not hamper display or enjoyment. That is a presentation qualification, not a blanket exemption.

Public-interest text has a different exception. The Article 50 label is not required where the content underwent human review or editorial control and a natural or legal person holds editorial responsibility. A casual human glance is not a useful control standard. The organisation should define the review, identify the accountable editor, and retain evidence that the process occurred.

## Build the Control From Trigger to Evidence

A practical implementation can follow nine steps:

1. **Inventory the use case.** Record the system, model, intended purpose, audience, modalities, delivery channels, markets, and whether output is used in the EU.
2. **Map the actor.** Identify the provider, deployer, importer, distributor, and downstream parties for this specific use. Reassess after rebranding, substantial modification, or intended-purpose changes.
3. **Test the four triggers.** Evaluate direct interaction, synthetic-content generation, emotion or biometric analysis, and publication of deepfakes or public-interest text separately.
4. **Separate technical and human controls.** Keep provider-side marking and detection requirements distinct from deployer-side notices and labels in requirements, contracts, and acceptance tests.
5. **Place the disclosure at the event.** Deliver clear, distinguishable, accessible information no later than first interaction or exposure in every relevant channel.
6. **Document exceptions.** Record the facts supporting obviousness, standard editing, human review, editorial responsibility, artistic treatment, or a statutory law-enforcement qualification.
7. **Allocate the value chain.** Use contracts to address preservation of marks, notice placement, system changes, evidence access, and escalation when a control fails.
8. **Retain evidence.** Keep design decisions, tests, screenshots or samples, accessibility checks, version history, approvals, and corrective actions.
9. **Reassess changes.** Repeat the analysis after material changes to the model, system, interface, intended purpose, audience, content workflow, or applicable standards.

## A Readiness Matrix

The strongest evidence connects the legal trigger to a product control and an accountable owner.

| Owner | Priority | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product and engineering | Build notices, machine-readable marking, detection, and mark-preservation requirements into supported channels | Product requirements, architecture decisions, acceptance tests, exported samples, version history |
| Legal and compliance | Classify roles and triggers; approve exceptions and disclosure language | Use-case assessment, legal mapping, exception rationale, approval record |
| Content and editorial | Govern deepfake and public-interest publication workflows | Label templates, review records, named editorial responsibility, publication samples |
| Procurement and vendor management | Allocate provider and deployer responsibilities across the value chain | Contract clauses, technical specifications, evidence-access rights, change notifications |
| Accessibility and user experience | Make information clear, distinguishable, timely, and accessible in each channel | Accessibility tests, screenshots, language variants, first-interaction or first-exposure tests |

## Article 50 Is Not the Whole Transparency Regime

Article 50 applies alongside other AI Act requirements.

For high-risk systems, providers must design sufficient transparency for deployers to interpret output and use the system appropriately. They must supply instructions covering intended purpose, performance, limitations, risks, interpretation, human oversight, maintenance, and logs. When the amended high-risk timelines apply, deployers have separate duties covering use, oversight, monitoring, log retention, workplace information, and notices to natural persons subject to certain Annex III decision systems.

GPAI-model providers have a different value-chain layer under Article 53. Their duties include technical documentation, information for downstream system providers, an EU-copyright compliance policy, and a public summary of training content using the AI Office template. Those model-level duties do not replace Article 50(2)'s output-marking requirement.

## The Transparency Code Is a Voluntary Route

The [Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-generated Content](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/code-practice-ai-generated-content) addresses provider marking and detection and deployer labelling of deepfakes and certain text. The Commission and AI Board have assessed the code as an adequate voluntary route for demonstrating compliance with Article 50(2), (4), and (5).

Adherence is not conclusive proof of compliance. Organisations may use alternative measures, but they must be able to demonstrate that those measures are adequate. The Article 50 transparency code is also separate from the GPAI Code of Practice supporting Articles 53 and 55.

## Enforcement Makes the Evidence Material

National market-surveillance authorities generally enforce Article 50. Under the amended Act, the AI Office has exclusive competence for specified systems under Article 75, subject to listed exceptions, while the European Data Protection Supervisor supervises Union institutions, bodies, offices, and agencies.

Non-compliance with Article 50 may attract administrative fines of up to **EUR 15 million** or, for an undertaking, **3% of total worldwide annual turnover** in the preceding financial year, whichever is higher. For SMEs, including start-ups, the lower of the percentage or fixed amount applies. Penalties remain subject to proportionality, procedural safeguards, and the applicable enforcement framework.

The practical response is to preserve evidence that the selected mechanism addressed the correct trigger, actor, channel, and audience at the required time, rather than to collect documents for their own sake.

## Audit One Production Use Case

For each system, record five fields:

1. **Actor:** Who is the provider and who is the deployer for this use?
2. **Trigger:** Which Article 50 situation applies?
3. **Mechanism:** What technical mark, notice, or label meets the duty?
4. **Exception:** Is one claimed, and what facts support it?
5. **Evidence:** What proves the mechanism worked in the relevant version and channel?

If those fields cannot be completed consistently across product, legal, engineering, and operations, the transparency control is not yet operational.
